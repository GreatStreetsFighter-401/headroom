# What each reform would do in Rhode Island — a plain-language comparison

*Draft v0.1, 2026-10-04, from the tier-B run (seed 20261216, 2,000 draws). Numbers are
permitted homes over 2027–2031, statewide, shown as a likely range (P10–P90) around a middle
estimate (P50). Page budget: 4.*

**The baseline.** With no new reform, Rhode Island is on course to permit roughly **18,600
homes** over the five years (likely range 16,200–21,400). That already clears the State's
Housing 2030 goal of 15,000, because 2025 was the strongest permit year since the 1980s and
the model carries part of that momentum forward. Whether 2025 was a new level or a peak is
the biggest single uncertainty in every number below.

## Accessory dwelling units

*Allow one ADU on every single-family lot, detached or attached, with no added parking.*

**Change:** about **+1,160 homes** (range +430 to +3,170).
**What drives it:** Rhode Island already allows an ADU by right on lots of 20,000 sq ft or
more and within an existing building, so the reform's effect is on the ~60 % of
single-family lots below that size, discounted where the lot is on septic. The number is
almost entirely the **uptake rate** — how many eligible owners build one each year — which
the model sets so that it reproduces the 163 ADUs Rhode Island permitted in 2024.
**How sure:** the range is wide (×7 top to bottom) because two years of state data cannot
pin the rate; the ramp speed is the second lever.
**What it cannot say:** who lives in them, or what towns' 800 sq ft caps do.

## Missing middle — two to six homes on a single-family lot

*Allow 2–6 units on lots now limited to one house, up to three stories.*

**Change:** about **+360 homes** (range +100 to +1,260).
**What drives it:** the stock of houses in single-family-only districts (read from the
ordinance where parsed, else from the State's district descriptions, else a statewide share),
times a **conversion rate** taken from Portland's and Minneapolis's experience (small
conversions are rare: a few tenths of a percent of lots a year at most), times the share of
lots where land is worth more than the house on it — the one number in the model still
flagged as a placeholder.
**How sure:** moderately; Minneapolis found most new homes came in 4+-unit buildings, which
this scenario excludes by definition.
**What it cannot say:** which neighbourhoods change, or whether existing residents move.

## Parking minimums reduced

*Cut or end minimum parking for new homes.*

**Change:** about **+200 homes** (range +70 to +500).
**What drives it:** a cost channel. A mandated space developers would not otherwise build
costs roughly $8,000–45,000; the saving is a few percent of a $400,000 unit; a supply
elasticity near 0.8 turns that into a few percent more multifamily permits. Near transit the
State already caps the mandate at one space (2026), so the reform's increment there is small.
**How sure:** the elasticity and the cost per space are both ranges from the literature, not
Rhode Island measurements; the required-spaces baseline is a placeholder until the 39
ordinances' parking tables are coded.
**What it cannot say:** where the saved money goes — rents, land, or more units.

## Development fees halved

*Cut planning, review, building and impact fees by half.*

**Change:** about **+820 homes** at the handbook's $50,000-per-unit baseline (range +430 to
+1,490) — but only **+140** (range +70 to +280) at Rhode Island's actual fee levels.
**What drives it:** the same cost channel. The difference between the two lines *is* the
finding: Rhode Island's fees are a fraction of the handbook's benchmark (building fees of
$10–15 per $1,000 of value, few impact fees), so fee relief buys little here while it would
buy a lot in a high-fee state.
**How sure:** the Rhode Island fee total is a placeholder until the fee schedules are
collected; the direction of the finding does not depend on it.
**What it cannot say:** anything about other states' fee levels.

## Transit-oriented density

*Allow more height and floor area near frequent transit, with less parking.*

**Change:** about **+340 homes** (range +110 to +950).
**What drives it:** residential land within ⅛ mile of a frequent stop or ¼ mile of a hub —
the distances Rhode Island wrote into law in 2026 — times the gap between a 20-home-per-acre
allowance and today's minimum-lot density, times a slow realisation rate: upzoned land near
transit builds out over decades, not years.
**How sure:** least sure of the five. "Frequent" is read from RIPTA's schedule (four
departures an hour at peak); "hub" is a proxy (six or more routes) until the Transit Master
Plan's list is encoded, and it finds downtown Providence and Pawtucket and little else.
**What it cannot say:** site assembly, infrastructure limits, design review.

## Reading the five together

* Every reform helps; none is transformative on its own over five years. The largest, ADUs,
  adds about six percent to the baseline.
* The handbook's fee scenario is the clearest case of a reform that matters elsewhere and not
  here; running it at local fee levels is the honest answer.
* The ranges are wide because the behavioural rates are the uncertainty, not the land: the
  land is counted, the owners' decisions are estimated. The sensitivity tables say so for
  every scenario.
* The three data tiers give nearly the same ranges for ADUs and missing middle, because the
  ordinance reading mostly refines *where* the homes land rather than how many; the transit
  scenario needs a zoning layer and cannot run from Census data alone.
